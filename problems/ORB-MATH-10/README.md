# ORB-MATH-10: Arithmetic nature of the odd Riemann zeta values: irrationality of $\zeta(2m+1)$ for $m\geq 2$

For the Riemann zeta function $\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s}$, the values at even integers are classical rational multiples of powers of $\pi$ and hence transcendental, and $\zeta(3)$ is irrational by Apéry's 1979 theorem. However, for every odd argument $2m+1$ with $m\geq 2$ — beginning with $\zeta(5)$ — the arithmetic nature of $\zeta(2m+1)$ (rational, irrational, or transcendental) is unknown. Modern collective results show that infinitely many odd zeta values are irrational (Rivoal; Ball–Rivoal; Fischler–Sprang–Zudilin; Zudilin), and that at least one of $\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\zeta(9),\zeta(11)$ is irrational, but no individual value beyond $\zeta(3)$ has ever been identified as rational or irrational. The problem is to determine the arithmetic nature of an individual odd zeta value $\zeta(2m+1)$, $m\geq 2$ — in the standard strong form, to prove that $\zeta(2m+1)$ is irrational for every $m\geq 1$. Since $\zeta(2m+1)^{k}$ is rational or irrational exactly when $\zeta(2m+1)$ is, this subsumes the arithmetic classification of all powers of odd zeta values, the question left open in the source context (Banerjee–Sahani, 2022). A systematic audit of the literature confirms the problem remains open.

## Background

The Riemann zeta function is defined for complex $s$ with $\mathrm{Re}(s)>1$ by the Dirichlet series $\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s}$. A real number is called irrational if it is not a ratio of integers, and transcendental if it is not a root of any nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients (transcendental numbers are in particular irrational). The arithmetic of zeta values — the question of which $\zeta(n)$ are rational, irrational, or transcendental — goes back to the eighteenth century. At even arguments a classical closed formula expresses $\zeta(2m)$ as a rational multiple of $\pi^{2m}$; since $\pi$ is transcendental, every even zeta value is transcendental. At odd arguments almost nothing unconditional is known about individual values. In 1979 Apéry stunned the community with a proof that $\zeta(3)$ is irrational, based on an explicit sequence of rational approximations converging to $\zeta(3)$ fast enough to force irrationality; this remains the only individual odd zeta value whose arithmetic nature is known. Subsequent progress has been collective rather than individual. Rivoal (2000) proved that infinitely many of the numbers $\zeta(3),\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\dots$ are irrational. Ball and Rivoal (2001) proved a quantitative version: the $\mathbb{Q}$-linear span (the vector space of rational linear combinations) of $1,\zeta(3),\zeta(5),\dots,\zeta(s)$ has dimension at least $\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\log 2}\log s$ for any $\varepsilon>0$ and $s$ large enough. Zudilin (2001) showed that at least one of the four numbers $\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\zeta(9),\zeta(11)$ is irrational; later refinements (Zudilin 2018 by elementary means; Lai–Zhou 2022 proving at least two of $\zeta(5),\dots,\zeta(35)$ irrational) sharpened the window, and Fischler, Sprang, and Zudilin (2019) proved that at least $2^{(1-\varepsilon)\log s/\log\log s}$ of the odd zeta values up to $\zeta(s)$ are irrational. These results rest on constructing explicit families of linear forms (rational linear combinations of zeta values and 1) with controlled denominators and using Nesterenko-type linear independence criteria — techniques that bound how many values are simultaneously independent but fundamentally cannot pin down any single value. The source context of this candidate is a 2022 paper of Banerjee and Sahani deriving Ramanujan-type transformation formulas for higher powers of odd zeta values (related to Lambert series, i.e. series of the shape $\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_n}{e^{2\pi n}-1}$) and generalized Eisenstein series (modular-form analogues of such series); that paper explicitly notes the arithmetic nature of odd zeta values is unresolved, and its identities yield only disjunctive statements, e.g. that for $m$ odd at least one of $\zeta(2m+1)$ and $\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{2m+1}(e^{2\pi n}-1)}$ is transcendental, never an unconditional statement about $\zeta(2m+1)$ alone.

## Problem Statement

Determine the arithmetic nature of the values of the Riemann zeta function at odd integers $2m+1$ with $m\geq 2$: prove that $\zeta(2m+1)$ is irrational — or rational — for at least one specific such value (starting with $\zeta(5)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-5}$), or in the standard strong form prove that $\zeta(2m+1)$ is irrational for every integer $m\geq 1$, of which only the case $m=1$ ($\zeta(3)$) is settled. A complete answer includes deciding the stronger question of whether such values are transcendental (not roots of any nonzero rational polynomial), since irrationality alone is expected to be strictly weaker. Because $\zeta(2m+1)^{k}$ is rational exactly when $\zeta(2m+1)$ is rational, an answer settles the arithmetic classification of all powers $\zeta^{k}(2m+1)$ and, jointly with the existing span-dimension theorems, would close the last gap in the collective theory of odd zeta values.

The verification contract below evaluates answers to this statement. It does not narrow or redefine the research question.

Known solving difficulties:

- All known techniques (Apéry-type recurrences, hypergeometric and Arnold-type hypergeometric integrals, Nesterenko linear-independence criteria, saddle-point asymptotics) yield only collective or disjunctive irrationality statements, and the field's consensus is that isolating one value requires a genuinely new approximation construction.
- Apéry-style approaches for $m\geq 2$ fail by a delicate margin: the known rational approximations to $\zeta(2m+1)$ do not converge fast enough relative to their denominator growth to force irrationality of a single value.
- Nesterenko-type criteria fundamentally measure dimensions of $\mathbb{Q}$-spans, not individual irrationality, so quantitative improvements along the Ball–Rivoal line cannot by themselves solve the problem.
- A rationality answer (some $\zeta(2m+1)$ rational) is considered extremely implausible and would contradict no theorem, but proving it would require completely different machinery (e.g. exact closed forms), none of which is known to exist.
- The transcendence strengthening is far beyond current tools: even for $\zeta(3)$, where irrationality is known, transcendence remains open, and no general transcendence method applies to individually odd zeta values.

## Current Progress

Banerjee–Sahani (arXiv:2206.13331) discuss the arithmetic nature of odd zeta values. The paper itself states that 'the arithmetic nature of zeta values at odd integral arguments is still far from being known', cites Apéry for $\zeta(3)$ and Zudilin for the $\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\zeta(9),\zeta(11)$ disjunction, and derives no unconditional arithmetic statement about any individual odd zeta value. However, the question is not posed by this paper; it is the classical open problem of the field, so the record is aligned with the standard formulation.

Apéry (Astérisque 61, 1979) proved $\zeta(3)$ irrational via explicit rational approximations (recurrences producing fractions $p_n/q_n$ with error smaller than $1/q_n^{1+\delta}$). This remains, as of August 2026, the only individual odd zeta value whose rationality or irrationality is known.

Rivoal (C. R. Acad. Sci. Paris 331, 2000) proved that infinitely many of $\zeta(3),\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\dots$ are irrational, and Ball–Rivoal (Invent. Math. 146, 2001, 193–207) proved $\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}\operatorname{span}\{1,\zeta(3),\dots,\zeta(s)\}\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\log 2}\log s$. These are dimension/collective statements; they do not identify any single $\zeta(2m+1)$, $m\geq 2$, as irrational.

Zudilin (Russ. Math. Surveys 56, 2001, 774–776; DOI 10.4213/rm427) proved at least one of $\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\zeta(9),\zeta(11)$ is irrational — the sharpest small-window disjunction, still leaving every individual value unresolved. Later refinements: Zudilin (SIGMA 14, 2018) gave an elementary proof that one of $\zeta(5)\dots\zeta(25)$ is irrational, and Lai–Zhou (Publ. Math. Debrecen 101, 2022, 353–372) proved at least two of $\zeta(5)\dots\zeta(35)$ are irrational.

Fischler–Sprang–Zudilin (Compositio Math. 155, 2019, 938–952; arXiv:1803.08905) proved that at least $2^{(1-\varepsilon)\log s/\log\log s}$ odd zeta values up to $\zeta(s)$ are irrational, the strongest quantitative collective result; Lai (arXiv:2407.14236, 2024) slightly improved the Ball–Rivoal dimension constant to $1.119\log s/(1+\log 2)$. The entire line remains collective: no individual value is pinned down, and the methods (hypergeometric constructions of linear forms plus Nesterenko-type criteria) are not expected by experts to settle a single value.

No accepted resolution for any individual $\zeta(2m+1)$ with $m\geq2$ was identified at the August 2026 curation cutoff. One unrefereed preprint (S. Suman, arXiv:2407.07121, 2024) claims an elementary Diophantine proof of the irrationality of $\zeta(5)$ and all higher odd zeta values; the claim matches a well-known genre of flawed elementary attempts, has no journal acceptance or community uptake in the literature discussed here, and is treated as non-resolving. The precise surviving open core is unchanged: for every integer $m\geq 2$, the rationality, irrationality, and transcendence of $\zeta(2m+1)$ (equivalently of $\zeta(2m+1)^{k}$ for any $k\geq 1$) are all open.

## Scientific Significance

Affected-field significance: `high`.

Direct impact on the core knowledge of transcendental number theory and the arithmetic study of special values of $L$-functions. Settling any individual case — e.g. proving $\zeta(5)$ irrational — would be the first identification of a new individual odd zeta value since Apéry (1979) and would almost certainly require a fundamentally new construction of rational approximations (linear forms) beyond the hypergeometric/Nesterenko-criterion toolkit that yields only collective results; such a method would change what is provable for related constants (Catalan's constant, multiple zeta values, Dirichlet $L$-values). Proving irrationality for all $m\geq 1$, or transcendence, would resolve a centuries-old question central to the field's understanding of zeta values.
