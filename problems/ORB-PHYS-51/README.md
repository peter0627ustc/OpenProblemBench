# ORB-PHYS-51: A fault-tolerance threshold theorem for adiabatic quantum computation under simultaneous decoherence and control errors

Adiabatic quantum computation (AQC) performs computation by slowly varying a local Hamiltonian whose ground state encodes the answer, but unlike the circuit model it has no threshold theorem: no proof that an adiabatic computer can carry out arbitrarily long computations reliably once noise and control imperfections are present. Lidar (2008) showed that a hybrid of dynamical decoupling, subsystem/stabilizer encoding, and energy-penalty gap protection makes the total AQC error arbitrarily small, but at the price of pulse intervals and widths that shrink as power laws in the problem size, with no correction of pulse imperfections and no threshold result. The open problem, audited against the primary source and the 2008–2026 literature, is to establish (or refute) an AQC analogue of the circuit-model threshold theorem: a protection architecture under which every polynomial-runtime adiabatic algorithm can be executed to arbitrarily small error with polynomial resource overhead, provided decoherence and control-error rates are below fixed constants. Partial results exist on each axis separately — encoded energy-penalty suppression of thermal errors, numerical dynamical decoupling, and threshold-type theorems for control errors in closed-system dynamics — but none combines decoherence, control noise, and polynomial overhead, so the problem remains open.

## Background

Adiabatic quantum computation (AQC) solves a problem by preparing the ground state of a simple initial Hamiltonian $H_0$ and slowly interpolating to a final Hamiltonian $H_1$ whose ground state encodes the answer, via $H_{\mathrm{ad}}(s)=(1-f(s))H_0+f(s)H_1$ with $s=t/T\in[0,1]$. The adiabatic theorem guarantees that the evolution stays near the instantaneous ground state provided $T$ is large compared with the inverse square (or cube, depending on smoothness) of the minimum spectral gap $\Delta$ between the ground state and the rest of the spectrum. For universal AQC this gap shrinks as an inverse polynomial in the problem size $n$, so the runtime $T$ grows polynomially. Aharonov, van Dam, Kempe, Landau, Lloyd, and Regev (2007) proved that AQC is polynomially equivalent to the standard circuit model, so AQC is in principle as powerful as gate-based quantum computing.

In the circuit model, fault-tolerance threshold theorems (beginning with Aharonov and Ben-Or, 1997) show that arbitrarily long quantum computations succeed with only polynomial overhead once physical error rates are below a fixed constant threshold, by combining error-correcting encodings with locality of faults. AQC lacks any analogous theorem. The difficulty is structural: computation is a continuous analog evolution, so there are no gates and no natural points at which to extract error syndromes; errors occur continuously during the interpolation; the energy gap that protects the computation shrinks polynomially with $n$, so thermal excitation and noise must be compared with a vanishing energy scale; and the standard universality construction (history states plus perturbative gadgets, reductions of many-qubit interaction terms to two-qubit terms via ancillas and energy penalties) introduces unprotected ancillas, so compiling a fault-tolerant circuit into a Hamiltonian does not by itself yield a fault-tolerant analog device.

Lidar (2008) introduced a hybrid protection methodology for AQC combining: (a) dynamical decoupling (DD) — applying strong, fast control pulses whose net effect averages out the system-bath interaction; the pulses are chosen to commute with the adiabatic Hamiltonian so they do not disturb the computation; (b) encoding of the logical qubits into subsystem and stabilizer codes — encodings in which the noiseless information lives in a factor of the Hilbert space (the algebraic complement, or commutant, of the algebra generated by the decoupling group) that the bath-induced errors cannot reach; and (c) energy-penalty (gap) protection in the sense of Jordan, Farhi, and Shor (2006), where terms are added to raise the energy of states outside the code space. He proved a total error bound $\delta_S \le d_D + \delta_{\mathrm{ad}}$, the sum of a decoupling error $d_D$ and the closed-system adiabatic error $\delta_{\mathrm{ad}}$, and showed that periodic DD (PDD) with pulse interval $\tau$ and width $w$ shrinking as power laws in $n$ ($\tau \sim n^{-(\zeta+\epsilon_1)}/\Delta_0$, $w \sim n^{-(2\zeta+\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2)}/J$, with $\Delta_0$ the Hamiltonian energy scale, $J$ the system-bath coupling strength, and $\zeta$ set by the gap-scaling exponent) makes the total error arbitrarily small. However, the paper explicitly concludes that a threshold theorem for fault-tolerant AQC is still missing, that achieving one will most likely require incorporating feedback to correct DD pulse imperfections and other control noise (suggesting syndrome measurements on the commutant factor as in subsystem-code circuit fault tolerance), that the shrinking-pulse requirement is a shortcoming expected — but not proven — to be removable by concatenated DD (nesting of pulse sequences that suppresses low-frequency noise to higher perturbative order), that energy-penalty protection typically requires $n$-local or more-than-two-body interactions that physical architectures cannot directly implement, and that noise acting on the interpolation function $f(s)$ or introducing independent time scales is unaccounted for.

## Problem Statement

Establish (or refute the possibility of) a fault-tolerance threshold theorem for adiabatic quantum computation, in the native analog Hamiltonian model, playing the role that circuit-model threshold theorems play for gate-based quantum computing. Specifically: exhibit a protection architecture for AQC — combining, for example, dynamical decoupling, subsystem or stabilizer encodings, energy-penalty gap protection, and possibly feedback or syndrome measurements on the commutant/subsystem factor — together with a proof that, under a specified local noise model for decoherence (system-bath coupling) together with a specified model of control errors (including dynamical-decoupling pulse imperfections such as finite pulse width and pulse-strength errors), every AQC algorithm whose runtime and minimum gap scale polynomially in the problem size $n$ can be executed with final error at most $\epsilon$ using resources (qubits, pulses, energy scales, total time) polynomial in $n$ and $1/\epsilon$, whenever the decoherence and control-error rates are below fixed positive constants. In accordance with the source, the answer should address the identified necessary ingredients: (i) how active correction of DD pulse imperfections and other control noise (e.g., feedback and syndrome extraction compatible with the ongoing adiabatic evolution) is integrated; (ii) whether the requirement that pulse intervals and widths shrink as power laws in $n$ can be removed, e.g., by a rigorous demonstration that concatenated dynamical decoupling achieves the needed suppression with non-vanishing pulse scales in the AQC setting; (iii) whether gap protection can be implemented with physically feasible, locality-preserving (two-body or otherwise architecturally admissible) interactions rather than $n$-local terms; and (iv) how noise acting on the interpolation function $f(s)$ or introducing additional independent time scales is handled. A negative answer — a proof that under some specified realistic local noise model no such threshold with polynomial overhead exists for analog AQC — would equally resolve the problem.

The verification contract below evaluates answers to this statement. It does not narrow or redefine the research question.

Known solving difficulties:

- No gate structure: errors occur continuously throughout an analog interpolation, so the circuit-model technique of associating faults with discrete gate locations and extracting syndromes between them has no direct counterpart; new ways to localize and correct errors during an uninterrupted evolution must be invented.
- Vanishing protection scale: the minimum spectral gap shrinks as an inverse polynomial in the problem size, so thermal excitation rates, control precision, and decoupling pulse scales must all be controlled relative to an energy scale that tends to zero with n.
- Correcting the corrector: dynamical-decoupling pulses are themselves controls with imperfections (finite width, strength and timing errors), and adding feedback or syndrome measurements risks disturbing the unknown adiabatic solution state; combining open-loop decoupling with active error correction for AQC without destroying coherence of the encoded ground-state computation is unresolved.
- Locality constraints: energy-penalty gap protection naturally requires many-body (n-local or k-local with k>2) interaction terms, while physical architectures provide few-body interactions, so locality-preserving or perturbative constructions with analyzable error must be found.
- Decoupling resource scaling: removing the requirement that pulse intervals and widths shrink as a power of the problem size, e.g., proving that concatenated decoupling sequences achieve the required suppression in the AQC setting while preserving commutation with the time-dependent adiabatic Hamiltonian, demands analysis beyond existing single-qubit or time-independent results.
- Imported-construction fragility: compiling fault-tolerant circuits through the universality construction introduces history states, perturbative gadgets, and unprotected ancilla qubits, so fault tolerance of the compiled Hamiltonian does not follow from the circuit threshold theorem and requires a substantially new argument.

Universality route (not a resolution): the equivalence of AQC with the circuit model (Aharonov et al., SIAM Journal on Computing 37, 166 (2007)) means a fault-tolerant circuit can in principle be compiled into an adiabatic evolution, but the compilation passes through history-state constructions and perturbative gadgets with unprotected ancilla qubits, so it does not deliver a threshold theorem for analog adiabatic hardware. Young, Sarovar, and Blume-Kohout (Physical Review X 3, 041013 (2013)) state explicitly that circuit-model error-handling routines do not transfer easily to AQC, confirming the field's reading.

Error-suppression line: Quiroz and Lidar (Physical Review A 86, 042333 (2012)) demonstrated numerically that high-order dynamical decoupling combined with a compatible encoding substantially improves open-system AQC fidelity. Young, Sarovar, and Blume-Kohout (Physical Review X 3, 041013 (2013)) and Sarovar and Young (New Journal of Physics 15, 125032 (2013)) unified energy-gap protection and dynamical decoupling in a single dynamical formalism and identified critical constraints and limitations of error suppression in AQC; none of these works proves a threshold.

Energy-penalty/encoding line: Bookatz, Farhi, and Zhou (Physical Review A 92, 022317 (2015)) proved that for Hamiltonian-based computation encoded with a single-qubit error-detecting code and one-local system-environment couplings, infinitely strong energy penalties completely suppress one-local errors, with bounds for finite penalties. Lidar (Physical Review A 100, 022326 (2019)) proved that encoded AQC protected by stabilizer subspace codes with an energy penalty growing only logarithmically in system size suppresses thermal excitations at arbitrary times for local Markovian environments and polynomial total evolution time, closing a question left open in earlier work. These are the strongest partial results on the decoherence axis, but they establish error suppression under specific noise models, not a fault-tolerance threshold with a fixed critical noise rate, and they do not treat control errors or feedback.

Control-error axis (partial): Okuyama, Ohki, and Ohzeki (Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A 380, 20210412 (2022)) and Okuyama and Ohzeki (arXiv:2301.13075 (2023)) proved threshold-type theorems for stochastic and deterministic analog control errors respectively, but only in isolated (closed-system) dynamics: if the noise strength is below the inverse computation time, a constant number of measurement repetitions recovers the target state. These results exclude decoherence — the central ingredient of the 2008 problem — and rest on measurement repetition rather than a fault-tolerant encoding with polynomial overhead.

Quantum annealing correction line: Pudenz, Albash, and Lidar (Nature Communications 5, 3243 (2014)) and subsequent nested quantum-annealing-correction and parity-architecture studies demonstrated by simulation and experiment that encoding plus energy penalties can improve annealing success rates, and Mohseni et al. (npj Quantum Information 7, 53 (2021)) showed that replacing individual qubits by qubit ensembles realizes an error-protected AQC Hamiltonian using only collective spin operators, partially addressing the n-locality problem for gap protection. All of these are suppression heuristics or specific constructions without threshold guarantees.

Digitized route (circumvention, not resolution): Barends et al. (Nature, 2016, DOI 10.1038/nature17658) implemented digitized adiabatic evolution on a gate-based superconducting processor, where circuit-model fault tolerance applies in principle; this sidesteps rather than solves the open problem for native analog adiabatic computation.

Review-level status: the comprehensive review by Albash and Lidar (Reviews of Modern Physics 90, 015002 (2018)) deliberately excludes open-system AQC 'with the associated questions of error correction and fault tolerance' as deserving a separate treatment, indicating the topic remained unsettled at review level in 2018. No threshold theorem covering both decoherence and control errors for fault-tolerant analog AQC was identified at the August 2026 curation cutoff.

These gaps do not plausibly hide a widely accepted resolution, since no review or later paper found refers to one.

## Scientific Significance

Affected-field significance: `high`.

A threshold theorem for fault-tolerant AQC would directly settle a core open question of quantum computing theory: whether the analog, Hamiltonian-based model of quantum computation is scalable under realistic noise, in the same way the circuit-model threshold theorems of the late 1990s established scalability of the gate model and underwrote the entire fault-tolerant gate-model roadmap. The impact is direct: it would change what the field knows about the power and limits of adiabatic quantum computers and quantum annealers (a hardware class built and sold commercially), determine whether their analog operation can in principle be preserved at scale or must be digitized, and supply concrete protection architectures and critical noise rates that hardware development could target. Conversely, an impossibility result would redirect the field away from analog scaling. Partial progress to date (encoded energy-penalty suppression, dynamical-decoupling fidelity gains, control-error thresholds) already shapes how annealing architectures are designed, indicating that the full theorem would be transformative for this subfield.
