# ORB-MATH-43: Existence of a polynomial pivot rule for the simplex method (the combinatorial core of Smale's Problem 9)

The simplex method solves a linear program by walking along edges of its feasible polytope, each step improving the objective; a pivot rule decides which step to take. It is unknown whether any pivot rule — deterministic or randomized, computable in polynomial time per step — always reaches an optimum after a number of pivots bounded by a polynomial in only the number of variables $n$ and constraints $m$, counting degenerate pivots. A positive answer with a strongly polynomially implementable rule would give the first strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming, resolving the ninth of Smale's problems for the twenty-first century; a negative answer would show that the simplex paradigm cannot be strongly polynomial. The problem is open: exponential pivot lower bounds are now known for the entire shadow-vertex family (every choice of projection), for steepest-edge variants (every norm), for Cunningham's rule, and for rank-comparison rule classes; subexponential lower bounds hold for the randomized RANDOM-EDGE and RANDOM-FACET rules; the randomized algorithm of Kelner and Spielman is polynomial only in the input bit-length; and the geometric prerequisite — a polynomial bound on the monotone diameter of polytopes, a strong form of the open polynomial Hirsch conjecture — remains unproved.

## Background

A linear program (LP) asks for the maximum of a linear objective $c^T x$ over a polytope $P=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n: Ax\le b\}$, the set of points satisfying $m$ linear inequalities. The simplex method solves an LP by moving from vertex to vertex of $P$ along edges, always to a neighbor with a larger objective value; since a polytope has finitely many vertices, such a monotone walk must end at an optimal vertex. A vertex is specified by a basis, a set of $n$ linearly independent constraints that are tight there, and one move is performed by a pivot: exchanging one constraint of the basis for one outside it. A pivot rule is the subroutine that decides, from the LP data and the current basis, which constraint enters and which leaves; its choices determine the number of pivots, which dominates the running time. A pivot is called degenerate when the exchange leaves the algorithm at the same vertex (possible when more than $n$ constraints are tight there); long runs of degenerate pivots at a single vertex are called stalling, and periodic ones cycling. Bland's rule — always enter the eligible constraint of smallest index, with a smallest-index tie-break on leaving — provably prevents cycling, but no rule is known that provably prevents stalling on general polyhedra.

All known polynomial-time algorithms for LP (ellipsoid and interior-point methods) are weakly polynomial: their running times depend polynomially on the bit-length $L$ of the input numbers. A strongly polynomial algorithm would perform a number of arithmetic operations bounded by a polynomial in $m$ and $n$ alone, independent of $L$. Whether LP admits such an algorithm is the ninth of the problems for the twenty-first century posed by Smale (1998), and the simplex method is the classical candidate: a pivot rule guaranteeing poly(m,n) pivots, with each rule step computable in strongly polynomial time, would settle it. The per-step arithmetic is not considered the obstruction — the quantities a pivot rule compares are ratios of subdeterminants of $A$ — the obstruction is purely combinatorial: nobody knows how to choose the pivots.

The question has two coupled sides. First, non-degenerate progress: any rule that always takes an improving edge requires, as a geometric prerequisite, that every polytope admit a monotone path of polynomial length from every vertex to an optimum for every linear objective — a polynomial bound on the monotone diameter, the worst-case length of a shortest monotone path. This is a strong form of the polynomial Hirsch conjecture: for any two vertices $u,w$ one can choose an objective whose unique optimum is $w$, so a polynomial monotone-diameter bound would imply a polynomial bound on the ordinary graph diameter of polytopes. The Hirsch conjecture itself (diameter at most $m-n$) is false — Santos constructed counterexamples — and the best general upper bounds on polytope diameters remain quasi-polynomial (Kalai and Kleitman, improved by Todd), so even the weaker, non-monotone polynomial bound is open. Second, degeneracy: via standard transformations that manufacture short paths at the price of heavy degeneracy (for example pyramidal extended formulations, higher-dimensional polytopes projecting onto the given one, whose apex is adjacent to all vertices of the base), solving general LPs in strongly polynomial time reduces to finding a pivot rule that prevents stalling and can be implemented in strongly polynomial time — an observation due to Megiddo (1986), recounted by Kukharenko and Sanità (2026). No such rule is known.

What is known is a growing list of exclusions. Klee and Minty (1972) deformed the $n$-cube so that Dantzig's original entering rule — choose the eligible variable with the most favorable reduced cost, the objective improvement per unit increase of that variable — visits exponentially many vertices. Exponential lower bounds are now known for most classical deterministic rules: Cunningham's least-recently-considered rule (Avis and Friedmann), greatest-improvement and steepest-edge variants for every choice of norm (Black), and, strikingly, the entire shadow-vertex family: the shadow rule chooses its path by lifting a path on the projection of the polytope onto a two-dimensional polygon, and Black constructs LPs on which every choice of projection yields exponentially many pivots; Black and Xue (2026) extend this to every coherent monotone path, meaning every monotone path realizable by such a projection. Zadeh's least-entered rule (enter the constraint that has so far been entered fewest times) was long the leading candidate for subexponential deterministic behavior; Friedmann claimed a subexponential lower bound for it (2011), Disser and Hopp found errors in that construction (2019), and Disser, Friedmann and Hopp then gave a new construction showing the rule is exponential in the worst case (2023) — though a 2025 preprint by Zadeh disputes the correctness of these constructions, so this particular rule's worst case should be viewed as contested. Randomization does not escape the barrier: Friedmann, Hansen and Zwick (STOC 2011) constructed LPs on which the two most-studied randomized rules, RANDOM-EDGE (enter a uniformly random eligible constraint) and RANDOM-FACET (recurse on a uniformly random facet), require subexponentially many pivots; indeed the worst-case expected pivot count of RANDOM-FACET is itself known to be subexponential, with matching upper and lower bounds. Most recently, Disser, Loho, Maat and Mosis (2025) unified these constructions into a framework classifying rules by the input information they may use, proving a superpolynomial lower bound for memory-based rules that compare only ranks of improving pivots (including Bland-type generalizations, via parity games) and a subexponential lower bound for memoryless rules that compare ranks, reduced costs, or objective improvements (via Markov decision processes); both results carry over to the simplex method.

On the positive side, shadow-type rules take polynomially many steps in expectation on random input models and in smoothed analysis (where the input is perturbed by small Gaussian noise), and Kelner and Spielman (2006) turned this into a randomized algorithm with expected running time polynomial in the input bit-length: they run the shadow simplex method on a polytope with a randomly perturbed right-hand side. But its pivot bound depends on $L$, not on $(m,n)$ alone, and its walk is on the perturbed polytope rather than the given one, so it does not answer the pivot-rule question. Genuinely strongly polynomial pivot bounds are known only for structured LPs: Black, De Loera, Kafer and Sanità give shadow- and steepest-edge variants that take only linearly many non-degenerate steps on 0/1-polytopes (polytopes whose vertex coordinates are all 0 or 1), and Kukharenko and Sanità (2026) give an antistalling rule that limits consecutive degenerate pivots to $n-m-1$ on any LP, which, combined with bounds of Kitahara and Mizuno (2013) on non-degenerate pivots, yields strongly polynomial total pivot counts for the natural LP formulations of bipartite matching, vertex-cover, edge-cover and stable-set problems, of the stable marriage problem, and of flow problems with bounded capacities (for general minimum-cost flows a strongly polynomial pivot sequence already follows from Orlin's primal network simplex). Their rule, however, must be guided by an improving feasible direction at the current vertex, which in general is as hard to compute as solving the LP itself. Two further 2026 results frame the difficulty: Black and Steiner proved that computing a shortest monotone path to the optimum of a linear program is NP-hard (already for fractional knapsack polytopes), so 'follow the shortest path' is not an available strategy; and Natura proved that the circuit analogue of the polynomial Hirsch conjecture is true — every polyhedron in standard form admits a monotone walk of length $O(m^2\log m)$ if steps are allowed in arbitrary circuit directions (extreme directions of the constraint system, which may cut through the interior rather than along edges) — showing that the remaining obstruction is specific to edge walks, that is, to simplex pivots.

In summary: the existence of short monotone paths on general polytopes is open; the existence of an antistalling rule for general polyhedra is open; every natural candidate rule family proposed over seventy years has been excluded by exponential or subexponential lower bounds; and the available non-constructive existence results do not compose into a rule. The question below asks for the rule — or a proof that none exists.

## Problem Statement

Does there exist a pivot rule for the simplex method with a polynomial worst-case pivot bound?

Specifically: is there a rule — deterministic, or randomized with the bound required in expectation — computable by an algorithm running in polynomial time per step, that maps the LP data $(A,b,c)$, the current basis, and optionally the execution history to the choice of entering and leaving constraints, such that for every feasible linear program with a bounded optimum, with $n$ variables and $m$ constraints, and every initial basic feasible solution, the simplex method terminates at an optimal solution after at most $p(m,n)$ pivots for some explicit polynomial $p$, counting degenerate pivots, with every pivot producing an objective-improving (possibly degenerate) direction? If the per-step computation is strongly polynomial, an affirmative answer yields a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming and resolves Smale's ninth problem affirmatively within the simplex paradigm. Any affirmative answer must in particular establish the geometric prerequisite that every polytope admits, for every linear objective, a monotone path of polynomial length from every vertex to an optimum.

A complete negative answer must prove that no such rule exists — for example, via an explicit infinite family of linear programs on which every computable rule in the stated class requires superpolynomially many pivots, or a superpolynomial lower bound on the monotone diameter of polytopes, which would refute all monotone (simplex) rules at once. Lower bounds that exclude only specific rule families — shadow rules, steepest-edge variants, rank-comparison rules, RANDOM-EDGE, RANDOM-FACET, and the like — do not answer the question.

The verification contract below evaluates answers to this statement. It does not narrow or redefine the research question.

Known solving difficulties:

- Two coupled subproblems must be solved at once: bounding non-degenerate progress (equivalent to a polynomial bound on the monotone diameter, a strong form of the open polynomial Hirsch conjecture) and provably preventing stalling at degenerate vertices; known techniques address at most one side (antistalling rules need guidance as hard as solving the LP; short-path existence results are non-constructive or walk on subproblems rather than the given polytope).
- The natural candidate space is nearly exhausted: exponential lower bounds cover the classical entering rules, Cunningham's rule, steepest-edge variants for every norm, all shadow/coherent rules, and rank-comparison classes; subexponential bounds cover RANDOM-EDGE and RANDOM-FACET. A positive rule likely must exploit global problem data in a qualitatively new way.
- The rule must also be efficiently computable per step, but computing a shortest monotone path is NP-hard (Black and Steiner 2026), so the natural 'follow the shortest path' strategy is unavailable even where short paths exist.
- A negative answer requires an unconditional superpolynomial lower bound against every computable rule simultaneously — or a superpolynomial monotone-diameter construction — far beyond current lower-bound techniques, which exclude one rule family per construction.
- Even the geometric prerequisite is unresolved in both directions: the Hirsch conjecture is false, yet no polynomial diameter bound is proved and no superpolynomial diameter lower bound is known, so neither the affirmative nor the negative side has a usable geometric foundation.
- Degeneracy makes the decision space exponentially large at a single vertex (exponentially many bases can represent one vertex), so a rule must make provably safe choices from local and historical information alone.

## Current Progress

- Status: `ready`

Source-fidelity verification: the candidate's four LKM-anchored papers were checked against primary sources and are correctly attributed — Forsgren and Wang (arXiv:1908.09735) on non-constructive short pivot sequences, Black (Mathematical Programming 2026, arXiv:2403.04886) on shadow and steepest-edge exponential lower bounds, Friedmann, Hansen and Zwick (STOC 2011) on subexponential lower bounds for randomized rules, and Kukharenko and Sanità (Mathematical Programming 2026) on antistalling rules for structured LPs. One nuance is corrected in this record: Forsgren and Wang's short sequence (length at most the minimum dimension of the constraint matrix) is not a monotone path on the original polytope — its intermediate points are optimal solutions of a primal–dual pair of sub-LPs of increasing dimension — so it does not show that every LP has a short monotone path; whether polynomial-length monotone paths always exist (polynomial monotone diameter) is itself open.

The problem is the combinatorial core of Smale's ninth problem (1998), which asks for a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming. Megiddo (1986) observed that, via transformations that manufacture short paths at the cost of degeneracy, strongly polynomial LP reduces to finding a pivot rule that prevents stalling and is implementable in strongly polynomial time; as recounted by Kukharenko and Sanità (2026), this reduction is standard, and no such rule is known.

Klee and Minty (1972) initiated the lower-bound program with deformed cubes on which the original most-favorable-reduced-cost entering rule visits exponentially many vertices. Later exponential lower bounds cover Cunningham's least-recently-considered rule (Avis and Friedmann 2017), steepest-edge variants for every norm, and the entire shadow-vertex family — every choice of projection (Black 2026, whose Theorem 1.1 gives, for each dimension $n$, a simple polytope with $3n$ facets on which every shadow path from a fixed vertex has length at least $2^n$); Black and Xue (2026) extend the shadow exclusion to every coherent, i.e. projection-realizable, monotone path. Black explicitly poses as open (Open Problem 3.4) whether any shadow rule is even weakly polynomial.

Zadeh's least-entered rule was the last prominent candidate for subexponential deterministic behavior: Friedmann claimed a subexponential lower bound (IPCO 2011), Disser and Hopp identified errors in that construction (IPCO 2019), and Disser, Friedmann and Hopp gave a new construction showing the rule is exponential in the worst case (Mathematical Programming 2023). Zadeh's own 2025 preprint disputes the correctness of these constructions, alleging flawed probability constraints and mismatched LP/MDP instances, so the worst case of this specific rule is currently contested. Either way, the status of one rule family does not decide the existence question.

Friedmann, Hansen and Zwick (STOC 2011) gave the first subexponential lower bounds for randomized pivot rules, constructing LPs on which RANDOM-EDGE and RANDOM-FACET require subexponentially many pivots; the worst case of RANDOM-FACET is known to be subexponential. Randomization alone therefore does not yield a polynomial rule, although no lower bound is known against arbitrary randomized rules.

Disser, Loho, Maat and Mosis (arXiv:2512.16684, December 2025) unify lower-bound constructions by classifying rules according to the input information they may use (rank comparisons, reduced costs, objective improvements, memory). They prove a superpolynomial lower bound for memory-based Bland-type rank-comparison rules (via sink parity games) and a subexponential lower bound for memoryless rank-comparison rules (via Markov decision processes), both carrying over to the simplex method. Their paper opens by stating that the existence of a polynomial pivot rule for LP, policy iteration, and strategy improvement are 'prominent open problems' in their respective fields.

Kelner and Spielman (STOC 2006) gave a randomized polynomial-time algorithm in the bit model that runs the shadow simplex method on a polytope with a randomly perturbed right-hand side; its expected pivot bound depends polynomially on the input bit-length $L$, not on $(m,n)$ alone, and its walk is not on the given polytope, so it neither provides a polynomial pivot rule nor settles Smale's ninth problem. Average-case and smoothed analyses of shadow-type rules (recounted in Black 2026) likewise bound pivots in terms of distributional parameters rather than $(m,n)$ alone.

For structured LPs, strongly polynomial pivot bounds do exist: Black, De Loera, Kafer and Sanità (2025) give shadow and steepest-edge variants taking linearly many non-degenerate steps on 0/1-polytopes; Kukharenko and Sanità (2026) prove an antistalling rule limiting consecutive degenerate pivots to $n-m-1$ on any LP, which combined with Kitahara and Mizuno's bounds (2013) gives strongly polynomial total pivot counts for the natural LP formulations of bipartite matching, vertex-cover, edge-cover and stable-set problems, of stable marriage, and of bounded-capacity flow problems (for general minimum-cost flows this follows from Orlin's 1997 primal network simplex). Their rule needs an improving feasible direction at each vertex — as hard as solving the LP in general — and they explicitly leave the extension to broader LP classes open.

Geometric status: the Hirsch conjecture is false (Santos 2012); the best general diameter bounds remain quasi-polynomial (Kalai and Kleitman 1992, improved by Todd 2014); the polynomial Hirsch conjecture is stated as open in Kukharenko and Sanità (2026), and the stronger monotone version needed for pivot rules is likewise open — Blanchard, De Loera and Louveaux (2021) show exponentially long monotone paths (longest paths) on many combinatorial polytopes, but no superpolynomial lower bound on shortest monotone paths is known. Black and Steiner (2026) prove that computing a shortest monotone path to the optimum (or the diameter of a simple polytope) is NP-hard, ruling out the 'follow the shortest path' strategy. Natura (2026) resolves the circuit analogue: every polyhedron admits a monotone circuit walk of length $O(m^2\log m)$, so the open obstruction is specific to edge walks, i.e. to simplex pivots.

Coverage and uncertainty: this audit swept the arXiv corpus (queries 'simplex pivot rule' — all 42 hits inspected, 'polynomial Hirsch' — 36 hits, 'strongly polynomial algorithm linear programming' — 71 hits, 'monotone diameter polytope', 'Kelner Spielman'), DBLP, Crossref, zbMATH and ECCC, inspected all four source papers plus eleven adjacent works in primary form, and found no claim of a polynomial pivot rule, of a strongly polynomial LP algorithm, or of a general impossibility theorem. Every recent treatment (Black 2026; Disser, Loho, Maat and Mosis 2025; Kukharenko and Sanità 2026; Black and Steiner 2026; Natura 2026) states the question, or a direct prerequisite, as open. Residual uncertainties: the contested status of the least-entered rule lower bound (Zadeh's 2025 dispute); the Friedmann–Hansen–Zwick STOC 2011 content was verified through metadata and secondary accounts (the ACM page is paywalled); and Forsgren and Wang (2019) appears to remain an arXiv preprint without a journal version. None of these uncertainties affects the openness of the main question.

## Scientific Significance

Affected-field significance: `high`.

Field: linear programming and the theory of optimization algorithms (with closely analogous open pivot-rule questions for policy iteration on Markov decision processes and strategy improvement for parity games, which the same lower-bound framework addresses). A positive answer — an explicit rule with a proved poly(m,n) pivot bound and strongly polynomial per-step computation — would directly give the first strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming, resolving Smale's ninth problem and removing the bit-length dependence shared by all known polynomial LP algorithms (ellipsoid, interior-point, and the randomized simplex-type algorithm of Kelner and Spielman); since LP is the computational backbone of operations research and of branch-and-bound for integer programming, this would directly change the field's core capability. A negative answer would equally directly change core knowledge: it would establish that the simplex paradigm cannot be strongly polynomial and disprove the strong (monotone) form of the polynomial Hirsch conjecture, redirecting algorithm design toward non-pivoting or circuit-based paradigms. Both impacts are direct, not indirect.
