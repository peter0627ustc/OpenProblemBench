# ORB-PHYS-53: Is the quantum capacity of a general quantum channel uncomputable?

The asymptotic quantum capacity $Q(\Phi)$ — the optimal rate at which qubits can be transmitted reliably through many uses of a noisy quantum channel $\Phi$ — is defined by a regularization (a limit over arbitrarily many channel uses) with no known single-letter formula. A 2026 preprint by Bhattacharyya, Mehta, and Zhao proves that approximating $Q(\Phi)$ is QMA-hard when the channel is specified by a quantum circuit, and that the one-shot zero-error classical capacity assisted by maximally entangled states is uncomputable (for classical-to-quantum channels with projective decoding); however, the computability of $Q(\Phi)$ itself for general channels remains unresolved. This problem asks for a resolution of that question: either a proof that the real-valued map $\Phi\mapsto Q(\Phi)$ is not computable from a computable description of $\Phi$, or a proof of computability (an algorithm, possibly with a convergence guarantee, that determines $Q(\Phi)$ to any specified precision).

## Background

A quantum channel is the most general noise process in quantum mechanics, mathematically a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) linear map $\Phi$ on density matrices; a quantum circuit description specifies $\Phi$ succinctly by a gate list, while a Kraus-operator or formula description gives it explicitly. The quantum capacity $Q(\Phi)$ is the asymptotic number of qubits per channel use that can be sent with vanishing error. Unlike Shannon's classical capacity, which is given by a single maximization of a mutual information, $Q(\Phi)$ is only known to equal the regularized coherent information: $Q(\Phi)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\max_{\rho}\, I_c(\rho,\Phi^{\otimes n})$, where the coherent information $I_c$ is an entropy difference optimized over input states and the limit exists by superadditivity (Lloyd; Shor; Devetak 2005). Two structural phenomena show that this regularization cannot be replaced by a fixed finite number of channel uses: superactivation, where two channels each with zero quantum capacity combine to give positive capacity (Smith–Yard 2008), and the result of Cubitt, Elkouss, Matthews, Ozols, Pérez-García, and Strelchuk (2015) that for every $n$ there exist channels whose capacity is undetectable from fewer than $n$ uses, so for every $n$ some channel needs more than $n$ uses. These results are widely read as strong evidence that $Q(\Phi)$ may be uncomputable — that no Turing machine can compute it from any computable description of the channel — but no proof either way is known. (Uncomputability is already known for one asymptotic capacity notion: Elkouss and Pérez-García (2018) proved that the capacity of classical channels with memory cannot be computed even to within constant precision, but their construction relies essentially on memory, and the memoryless quantum case has remained open.) QMA is the quantum analogue of NP (quantum Merlin–Arthur verification), and RE is the class of recursively enumerable languages (those recognized by a Turing machine that halts on yes-instances); RE-hardness of a decision problem implies undecidability. The relevant undecidability machinery comes from nonlocal games — cooperative games in which separated players answering questions may share entanglement: Slofstra (2019) proved that deciding whether such a game admits a perfect quantum strategy is undecidable, and Ji, Natarajan, Vidick, Wright, and Yuen (MIP$^*$=RE, 2021) strengthened this to RE-completeness. Building on this, Bhattacharyya, Mehta, and Zhao (arXiv:2601.22471, 2026; v3 of March 2026 is the current version) initiated the study of the hardness of computing quantum channel capacities. They proved two results. First (their Theorem 1.1, formalized as Corollary 3.13): given a channel $\Phi_\mathcal{C}$ presented by a quantum circuit, it is QMA-hard to decide whether $Q(\Phi_\mathcal{C})\ge 3/4$ or $Q(\Phi_\mathcal{C})\le 1/4$, promised one holds. Second (their Theorem 4.1, restated as Theorem 1.3, with Corollary 4.2, restated as Theorem 1.2): for the one-shot zero-error classical capacity assisted by a maximally entangled state with projective decoding — denoted $C^{(1)}_{0,\mathrm{PME}}(\Phi)$ in the paper, and defined for classical-to-quantum channels (channels with a classical input alphabet and quantum outputs) — there is a computable mapping from graphs $G$ to channels $\Phi_G$ such that $C^{(1)}_{0,\mathrm{PME}}(\Phi_G)=\log(\alpha_q(G))$, where $\alpha_q(G)$ is the quantum independence number, the largest $t$ for which the independent-set nonlocal game on $G$ admits a perfect quantum strategy (Mančinska, Roberson, Varvitsiotis 2016); consequently, deciding $C^{(1)}_{0,\mathrm{PME}}(\Phi)\ge\log t$ is RE-hard, i.e., uncomputable. Zero-error capacity, introduced classically by Shannon (1956) and extended to quantum channels via noncommutative confusability graphs by Duan, Severini, and Winter (2013), measures perfect transmission; its assisted variant allows sender and receiver to share entanglement beforehand. Thus a one-shot, maximally-entanglement-assisted, zero-error capacity is provably uncomputable, while for the central capacity $Q(\Phi)$ only finite-complexity hardness (QMA) is known — a gap analogous to the historical progression for nonlocal games from hardness results toward MIP$^*$=RE. Notably, since uncomputability is encoding-independent in the zero-error case, the authors' open question also concerns whether hardness of $Q$ is robust across natural computable encodings (circuit versus explicit Kraus-operator descriptions). A related open question they highlight — already raised for classical channels by Mančinska and Roberson (2016) — is the computability of the zero-error classical capacity when arbitrary (not necessarily maximal) preshared entangled states are allowed; their reduction inherently uses maximal entanglement, and Stahlke (2016) showed that there exist quantum channels whose one-shot entanglement-assisted zero-error capacity is achievable only with non-maximally entangled assistance.

## Problem Statement

Determine the computability status of the asymptotic quantum capacity of general quantum channels. Precisely: given a quantum channel $\Phi$ specified by a natural computable description, with one such encoding fixed and stated explicitly (e.g., a quantum circuit implementing $\Phi$, or an explicit computable list of Kraus operators), determine whether there exists a Turing machine that, on input such a description and a precision $\varepsilon>0$, outputs a value within $\varepsilon$ of $Q(\Phi)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\max_{\rho} I_c(\rho,\Phi^{\otimes n})$ — and settle the question by proving one of the two branches: (a) a theorem entailing that the real-valued map $\Phi\mapsto Q(\Phi)$ is not computable under that stated encoding, for example by proving undecidability of distinguishing $Q(\Phi)\ge a$ from $Q(\Phi)\le b$ for fixed constants $a>b\ge 0$, under the promise that one of these alternatives holds; or (b) a computability theorem, giving an algorithm together with a proven termination/accuracy guarantee valid for all computably described channels in that encoding. This is the principal open problem left by Bhattacharyya, Mehta, and Zhao (arXiv:2601.22471), whose QMA-hardness result for circuit-specified channels is the strongest known lower bound (their paper shows the gapped decision problem lies in RE and conjectures it is RE-complete); a complete answer either strengthens that hardness to uncomputability or overturns the prevailing expectation. A directly related open problem from the same work, which a solver may additionally address as part of the same objective of mapping the computability boundary of quantum channel capacities, is the computability of the zero-error classical capacity assisted by arbitrary (not necessarily maximally) entangled preshared states.
