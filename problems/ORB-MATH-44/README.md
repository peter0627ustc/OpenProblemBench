# ORB-MATH-44: The 4/3-Conjecture for the Integrality Gap of the Subtour (Held–Karp) Relaxation of the Metric Traveling Salesman Problem

The subtour relaxation (subtour LP, or Held–Karp relaxation) is the standard linear programming relaxation of the symmetric traveling salesman problem, built from degree constraints and subtour-elimination constraints. Its integrality gap for metric instances — the worst-case ratio of the length of an optimal tour to the LP optimum — is known to be at least $4/3$, and the long-standing four-thirds conjecture asserts that it is exactly $4/3$. The best proven upper bound is still essentially $3/2$, improved only to $3/2-\epsilon$ for a tiny fixed $\epsilon>0$. Cook, Hougardy, and Petrich (arXiv:2603.12995) pushed exact computational verification to all instances on $n\le15$ vertices (maximum gap $11/9$, attained at the extreme points conjectured by Benoit and Boyd) and to all half-integral vertices for $n\le17$ (maximum $53/43$), while correcting earlier published extreme-point catalogs. This record asks for a resolution of the conjecture itself: prove $\mathrm{OPT}\le\frac{4}{3}\,\mathrm{LP}$ for every metric instance, or refute it with a metric instance, or family, whose ratio provably exceeds $4/3$. The source paper's agenda of extending exact, independently verifiable verification beyond $n=15$/$n=17$ is described as the derived computational problem it is.

## Background

The symmetric traveling salesman problem (TSP) asks for a shortest closed tour visiting each of $n$ vertices exactly once, given pairwise edge costs. In the metric TSP the costs are symmetric and satisfy the triangle inequality. The standard linear programming relaxation — the subtour relaxation, also called the subtour LP or Held–Karp relaxation — minimizes $\sum_e c_e x_e$ subject to the degree constraints $x(\delta(v))=2$ for every vertex $v$ (each vertex is entered and left exactly once; $\delta(v)$ is the set of edges incident to $v$ and $x(\delta(v))$ is the sum of the corresponding variables) and the subtour-elimination constraints $x(\delta(S))\ge 2$ for every nonempty proper vertex subset $S$ (a tour must cross every cut at least twice), with $x\ge 0$. Its optimum is a polynomial-time-computable lower bound on the length of an optimal tour, since the exponentially many subtour-elimination constraints can be separated by minimum-cut computations.

The integrality gap of this relaxation is the worst-case ratio $\mathrm{OPT}/\mathrm{LP}$ between the length of an optimal tour and the LP optimum, taken over all metric instances. Explicit metric constructions show the gap is at least $4/3$, so the bound $4/3$, if achievable, is tight. The four-thirds conjecture (also called the 4/3-conjecture) asserts that the gap is exactly $4/3$, i.e. that $\mathrm{OPT}\le\frac{4}{3}\,\mathrm{LP}$ for every metric instance. The conjecture dates back to the early 1990s — D. P. Williamson's 1990 MIT thesis on the Held–Karp heuristic is an early source, and Goemans (1995) surveys the early comparisons of valid inequalities — and is one of the most prominent open problems in combinatorial optimization. The best proven upper bound is still essentially $3/2$, from the classical analysis of Christofides' spanning-tree-plus-matching algorithm; Karlin, Klein, and Oveis Gharan improved it to $3/2-\epsilon$ for a tiny fixed $\epsilon>0$ (about $10^{-36}$ in their randomized analysis, with a smaller explicit improvement in the deterministic version).

Because the LP optimum is attained at an extreme point (vertex) of the subtour polytope $S^n$ — the set of feasible points of the relaxation on $n$ vertices — the worst-case ratio for instances on $n$ vertices can be computed exactly by enumerating these vertices: Benoit and Boyd (2008) showed that for a vertex $x$ its worst-case gap $\mathrm{gap}^+(x)=\max_c \mathrm{OPT}(c)/(c\cdot x)$ over metric cost functions $c$ is the reciprocal of the optimum of a single linear program minimizing $c\cdot x$ over the metric cone (triangle inequalities, nonnegative costs) subject to every tour having length at least $1$; the maximum of $\mathrm{gap}^+(x)$ over all non-isomorphic vertices of $S^n$ equals the exact integrality gap for $n$ vertices. This reduces finite verification of the conjecture to vertex enumeration — but the number of non-isomorphic vertices grows explosively: about $3.2\times10^8$ at $n=15$ in the general case, and $4.2\times10^7$ half-integral vertices at $n=17$ (half-integral vertices are those whose edge values all lie in $\{0,\frac{1}{2},1\}$, a structured subclass tied to cycle covers that plays a central role in TSP approximation).

Computationally, Benoit and Boyd (2008) verified the conjecture for all instances up to $n=10$ and conjectured which extreme points attain the maximum gap in general; Boyd and Elliott-Magwood (2010) extended the enumeration to $n=12$ (general) and $n=14$ (half-integral), and Boyd's 2011 web page published the vertex lists for $6\le n\le12$. Cook, Hougardy, and Petrich (2026) re-ran and extended this program with modern tools (nauty for support-graph generation, the Parma Polyhedra Library for vertex enumeration, cutting-plane gap LPs with exact rational verification): they found the previously published vertex lists were incomplete (1 vertex missing at $n=11$, 22 at $n=12$, and 5 and 18 half-integral vertices at $n=13$ and $n=14$), enumerated all vertices for $n\le15$ in the general case (maximum gap $11/9\approx1.2222$ at $n=15$) and all half-integral vertices for $n\le17$ (maxima $27/22\approx1.2273$ at $n=16$ and $53/43\approx1.2326$ at $n=17$), and published the full lists on the bonndata repository. In every case the maximum is still uniquely attained at the extreme points conjectured by Benoit and Boyd, so the observed maxima trace the conjectured worst-case family towards $4/3$ without exceeding it.

The conjecture is proven only for special classes: cubic and subcubic graph metrics (Boyd, Sitters, van der Ster, and Stougie 2014), half-integral cycle-cut instances — instances whose LP optimum is half-integral and whose support admits a certain laminar cut structure (Jin, Klein, and Williamson 2025) — and instances whose optimal LP solution has at most $n+6$ nonzero components (Villa, Vercesi, Barta, and Mastrolilli 2025). On the algorithmic side, the max-entropy rounding algorithm — which converts the fractional LP solution into a tour by sampling a spanning tree from the maximum-entropy distribution over the spanning trees contained in the LP support and adding a minimum matching on the resulting odd-degree vertices — powers the only better-than-$3/2$ approximation, yet it has a proven lower bound of $11/8$ on its approximation ratio and is a $10/7$-approximation on half-integral cycle-cut instances (both Jin, Klein, and Williamson 2026), so no current rounding analysis reaches $4/3$ on general instances.

## Problem Statement

Prove or refute the 4/3-conjecture for the symmetric metric traveling salesman problem.

For a finite vertex set $V$ and a symmetric cost function $c$ on the edges of the complete graph on $V$ satisfying the triangle inequality, let $\mathrm{OPT}(V,c)$ be the minimum length of a Hamiltonian cycle (tour) and $\mathrm{LP}(V,c)$ the optimum of the subtour relaxation

$$
\min \sum_{e} c_e x_e \quad\text{s.t.}\quad x(\delta(v))=2\ \ \forall v\in V,\qquad x(\delta(S))\ge 2\ \ \forall\,\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq V,\qquad x\ge 0.
$$

The conjecture asserts that $\mathrm{OPT}(V,c)\le\frac{4}{3}\,\mathrm{LP}(V,c)$ for every finite vertex set $V$ and every such cost function $c$; since constructions are known for which the ratio is at least $\frac{4}{3}$, a proof pins the integrality gap at exactly $\frac{4}{3}$.

An accepted answer must do exactly one of the following.

(a) Prove the inequality for all metric instances and all numbers of vertices. Any computer-assisted step in the proof must be independently replayable. A bound weaker than $\frac{4}{3}$, or a proof restricted to a subclass (half-integral solutions, bounded support size, graph metrics, cycle-cut structure, etc.), does not answer this problem.

(b) Refute the conjecture: exhibit one concrete metric instance whose exactly certified ratio $\mathrm{OPT}/\mathrm{LP}$ is strictly greater than $\frac{4}{3}$, or an explicit infinite family of metric instances whose ratios are provably bounded below by $\frac{4}{3}+\delta$ for some fixed $\delta>0$.

The source paper (Cook–Hougardy–Petrich, arXiv:2603.12995) additionally poses, as a derived computational problem, the extension of exact, independently verifiable verification beyond the current frontier — all extreme points for $n\le15$ in the general case, half-integral extreme points for $n\le17$ — including completing the published catalogs of subtour-polytope extreme points at larger $n$ and cross-checking them with independent enumeration engines. That extension strengthens the finite evidence but does not by itself answer this problem: in the source's own words, the exhaustive enumerations up to moderate $n$ do not constitute a proof for all $n$.

The verification contract below evaluates answers to this statement. It does not narrow or redefine the research question.

Known solving difficulties:

- The conjecture has been open for over three decades and the best proven upper bound is still essentially $3/2$ (improved only to $3/2-10^{-36}$), so a proof must close a large quantitative gap that all known rounding and dual-fitting techniques have failed to close.
- Exhaustive computational verification cannot reach all $n$: the number of non-isomorphic extreme points grows explosively (about $3.2\times10^8$ at $n=15$ in the general case), and independent cross-checking with alternative enumeration engines already becomes infeasible beyond $n=12$–$13$.
- Known rounding analyses are provably insufficient: the max-entropy algorithm, the engine of the only better-than-$3/2$ result, has a certified lower bound of $11/8$ on its approximation ratio.
- A refutation would require instances stronger than all known lower-bound families; systematic searches — exact enumeration up to $n=15$ (general) and $n=17$ (half-integral), and branch-and-cut exploration of the metric polytope — have found none, with the observed maxima growing slowly ($11/9$ at $n=15$).
- Structural theory of subtour-polytope extreme points is incomplete: existing structure theorems cover only restricted classes (cubic and subcubic graphs, cycle-cut instances, support size at most $n+6$), and connecting arbitrary vertex structure to tour optimality requires new mathematical tools.

## Current Progress

- Status: `ready`

Source-fidelity audit: the candidate's text was extracted from v1 (13 March 2026) of Cook–Hougardy–Petrich (arXiv:2603.12995) and reports the verification frontier as $n=14$ (general) and $n=16$ (half-integral), with the exact gap LP for $n=17$ half-integral reported too slow. In the current version v4 (7 July 2026), inspected in full for this audit, the authors completed those computations: all extreme points are enumerated for $n\le15$ in the general case (maximum gap $11/9\approx1.2222$; 319,185,400 non-isomorphic extreme points at $n=15$) and all half-integral extreme points for $n\le17$ (maxima $27/22\approx1.2273$ at $n=16$ and $53/43\approx1.2326$ at $n=17$), the maximum being still uniquely attained at the extreme points conjectured by Benoit and Boyd. Apart from this version lag the LKM paraphrase is faithful, and its central claim — the 4/3-conjecture is open in general — is correct.

Lineage of exact verification: Benoit and Boyd (2008) introduced the extreme-point enumeration framework (using PORTA), verified the conjecture for all instances up to $n=10$, and conjectured the worst-case extreme points; Boyd and Elliott-Magwood (2010) extended enumeration to $n=12$ (general) and $n=14$ (half-integral) via edge-splitting operations, and Boyd's 2011 web page published the vertex lists for $6\le n\le12$. Cook–Hougardy–Petrich (2026), replacing PORTA by PPL on modern hardware with nauty graph generation and cutting-plane gap LPs with exact rational verification, showed those published lists were incomplete (1 extreme point missing at $n=11$, 22 at $n=12$; 5 and 18 half-integral extreme points at $n=13$ and $n=14$), extended the enumeration to $n=15$/$n=17$, and cross-checked with two independent extreme-point tests, an independent isomorphism test, and re-runs with lrslib (up to $n=12$) and Normaliz (up to $n=13$); the corrected lists are published on bonndata (doi:10.60507/FK2/JK95PC, https://bonndata.uni-bonn.de/catalog/datasets/10.60507/FK2/JK95PC/).

Partial confirmations, none settling the general case: the bound $4/3$ is proven for cubic and subcubic graph metrics (Boyd–Sitters–van der Ster–Stougie 2014), for half-integral cycle-cut instances via a 4/3-approximation algorithm (Jin–Klein–Williamson 2025, Mathematical Programming 210), and for instances whose optimal LP solution has at most $n+6$ nonzero components (Villa–Vercesi–Barta–Mastrolilli, arXiv:2507.07003, v2 November 2025). The last is the closest recent structural advance and still leaves the general conjecture untouched.

Bound status: the gap is known to be at least $4/3$ through explicit constructions (Goemans 1995 surveys the early comparisons; Zhong 2025 gives the strongest current lower-bound constructions for the subtour LP ratio), while the upper bound remains essentially $3/2$, improved only to $3/2-\epsilon$ for a tiny fixed $\epsilon>0$ by Karlin–Klein–Oveis Gharan (deterministic version, IPCO 2023). Moreover, the max-entropy rounding algorithm behind that improvement has a proven lower bound of $11/8$ on its approximation ratio (Jin–Klein–Williamson 2026, Mathematical Programming 216) and is a $10/7$-approximation on half-integral cycle-cut instances (Jin–Klein–Williamson, arXiv:2607.01536, July 2026), so current algorithm analyses cannot reach $4/3$ in general; computational searches for larger-gap instances by branch-and-cut exploration of the metric polytope (Vercesi–Gualandi–Mastrolilli–Gambardella) have not exceeded the known families.

Very recent adjacent work still treats the conjecture as open: Yamanaka (arXiv:2511.11215, v2 December 2025) proves only a conditional transfer principle — if instances exist where the 2-edge-connected multisubgraph problem has a unique Hamiltonian-cycle integer optimum together with a half-integral LP solution, then the TSP integrality gap is at most $4/3$ — and explicitly states that constructing such instances remains open, noting that determining the integrality gap remains a long-standing open problem.

Coverage of this audit: same-core LKM semantic search (located the source, Cook–Hougardy–Petrich arXiv:2603.12995, Villa et al., Yamanaka, Jin–Klein–Williamson 2025/2026, Zhong, Vercesi et al., and the Benoit–Boyd / Boyd–Elliott-Magwood lineage), arXiv full-text search for subtour integrality-gap papers (no resolution claim found through August 2026), Crossref verification of every cited reference, and a Semantic Scholar citation query for arXiv:2603.12995 (no indexed citations yet — the paper is roughly five months old). No work claiming to prove or refute the general 4/3-conjecture was found. The open core is the conjecture itself for unrestricted metric instances; the derived computational frontier is $n\ge16$ in the general case and $n\ge18$ for half-integral vertices.

## Scientific Significance

Affected-field significance: `high`.

Solving the conjecture would directly change core knowledge in approximation algorithms and polyhedral combinatorics. A proof would pin the exact worst-case quality of the Held–Karp subtour relaxation, the central LP model of the TSP, and — since the relaxation is polynomial-time solvable and the known lower-bound constructions show $4/3$ is the best achievable LP-relative guarantee — a constructive proof is the natural route to a $4/3$-approximation algorithm for the metric TSP, breaking the decades-old $3/2$ barrier by far more than the current $3/2-10^{-36}$. A refutation would equally change core knowledge: it would prove that the subtour LP cannot certify a $4/3$ bound and that every analysis anchored to it is fundamentally limited, redirecting the field to stronger relaxations. Either answer also directly advances the polyhedral understanding of the subtour polytope's extreme points, on which the computational verification program of Benoit–Boyd, Boyd–Elliott-Magwood, and Cook–Hougardy–Petrich is built. The impact is direct, not indirect.
